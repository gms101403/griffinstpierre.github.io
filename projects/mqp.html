Major Qualifying Project

Pickleball Biomechanics MQP

Development of a wearable biomechanical system for measuring loaded ankle dorsiflexion during pickleball, providing kinematic data that may help identify potentially concerning movement patterns.

Role

Mechanical / Biomedical Engineering

Project Type

Focus

Biomechanics & Wearable Sensors

Tools

Overview

Project Overview

Pickleball involves rapid changes in direction and repeated lower-extremity loading that may contribute to injury risk. Our project responded to a client need for a non-invasive wearable system capable of collecting useful biomechanical data for injury-prevention research.

The original concept included multiple IMUs and force sensing. As the project progressed, the scope was narrowed to loaded ankle dorsiflexion, allowing the team to develop and validate a focused measurement system within the available project constraints.

The final system used a BNO085 IMU mounted in a custom shank hub and connected to electronics housed in a 3D-printed heel cuff. The system measured tibia-over-ankle angle during a controlled forward-lean movement, with MATLAB used to process the sensor data and calculate range of motion.

Client Need

Design Objective

"Design a non-invasive wearable device that provides useful kinematic data for pickleball players to help with injury prevention."

The final system therefore combined mechanical design, wearable sensing, signal processing, validation, and population-level analysis into a single measurement system.

Engineering Challenge

From Biomechanical Question to Measurement

The project focused on loaded ankle dorsiflexion, defined here as forward progression of the tibia over a planted foot. Tibia-over-ankle angle was used as the measurable quantity, with range of motion calculated between neutral standing and peak controlled dorsiflexion.

The final testing protocol required the foot to remain planted while the participant leaned forward. This was critical because the single-IMU system was designed specifically to measure sagittal-plane tibia-over-ankle motion. Foot movement or substantial side-to-side motion could introduce error.

A reference range of approximately 15–30° was used to provide context for the measurements. The system was intended as a measurement and research tool rather than a diagnostic device.

System Design

Designing the Wearable System

System Architecture

The original concept used two IMUs and force sensing to characterize movement and loading during pickleball. The final design focused on sagittal-plane dorsiflexion using a single BNO085 IMU positioned along the lower leg.

Heel Cuff Development

I designed and iterated the heel cuff in SolidWorks to securely house the electronics while maintaining a consistent connection to the foot. Iterations addressed fit, component separation, wiring access, manufacturability, and assembly.

Shank Hub Development

The shank hub provided the BNO085 mounting interface and a consistent sensor location along the lower leg. Iterations addressed strap alignment, enclosure access, attachment, structural stability, and assembly.

Prototyping & Development

From CAD to Physical Prototype

Mechanical components were developed through iterative CAD and FDM 3D printing. Physical prototypes were used to evaluate fit, sensor positioning, assembly, structural stability, and integration before participant testing.

Measurement Method

Measuring Loaded Ankle Dorsiflexion

Each participant completed a standing calibration followed by five controlled forward-lean trials on each ankle. The heel remained planted while the participant progressed the tibia forward over the foot.

The BNO085 recorded orientation data at approximately 100 Hz. A custom MATLAB GUI filtered the signal and identified the neutral and peak tibial positions for each trial. ROM was calculated from the angular difference between these positions.

Data Collection

Comparing Ankle Motion Across Populations

Following system development and initial testing, measurements were collected from four populations: college-aged men, college-aged women, men age 55+, and women age 55+. Fifteen participants were included in each group, with five trials collected for each ankle.

Research Questions

Hypotheses & Statistical Analysis

Hypothesis 1 — Limb Symmetry

Paired-samples t-tests found no statistically significant left-right differences within any population using α = 0.00833.

  • Men 55+: p = 0.5844
  • Women 55+: p = 0.6402
  • College Men: p = 0.8866
  • College Women: p = 0.8760

Conclusion: The null hypothesis was not rejected, supporting generally similar ROM between limbs.

Hypothesis 2 — Population Differences

Independent-samples t-tests identified statistically significant differences in every population comparison except Men 55+ versus Women 55+.

  • College Men vs. College Women: p = 0.0013
  • College Men vs. Women 55+: p < 0.0001
  • College Men vs. Men 55+: p < 0.0001
  • College Women vs. Women 55+: p = 0.0003
  • College Women vs. Men 55+: p = 0.0013
  • Men 55+ vs. Women 55+: p = 0.9235

Conclusion: The population-difference null hypothesis was rejected. College-aged men demonstrated the highest ROM, while both 55+ groups demonstrated substantially lower values. The difference between men and women was significant in the college-aged groups but not the 55+ groups.

Validation & Results

Evaluating the System

Before population testing, wearable measurements were compared against sagittal-plane video measurements obtained using Kinovea. The wearable system demonstrated an average difference of approximately 1.75° from the reference measurements.

Validation was limited by camera perspective and occasional side-to-side participant movement, which made identification of the peak sagittal-plane position less precise.

Repeatability

Five trials per ankle also allowed trial-to-trial consistency to be evaluated. Mean coefficients of variation ranged from 5.90–9.15% for the left ankle and 5.10–7.26% for the right ankle across the four populations.

Results

What We Found

The results showed a clear relationship between population and dorsiflexion ROM. College-aged men demonstrated the highest average ROM, followed by college-aged women, while both 55+ groups demonstrated substantially lower values.

Highest Mean ROM

College Men — 37.8° left / 36.7° right.

College Sex Difference

College men averaged approximately 6.6° more ROM.

Age-Related Change

Both sexes showed substantially lower ROM in the 55+ groups.

Limb Symmetry

No significant left-right differences were observed.

Mean symmetry values ranged from 8.98% in college men to 11.17% in college women. Overall, the population differences were driven by demographic group rather than consistent left-right differences.

Engineering Lessons

What I Learned

The project reinforced that a useful measurement depends on both the sensing technology and the mechanical system that positions it. Consistent sensor placement and a controlled testing protocol were essential to obtaining meaningful data.

It also demonstrated the iterative nature of engineering development. Mechanical design, prototyping, sensor integration, testing, and analysis continuously informed one another rather than functioning as isolated stages.

Limitations

System & Study Limitations

  • Sagittal-plane measurement: The final system did not directly measure three-dimensional relative motion.
  • Planted-foot requirement: Foot movement or substantial side-to-side motion could introduce measurement error.
  • Controlled movement: Testing did not reproduce the full complexity of live pickleball movement.
  • Validation: Kinovea measurements were affected by camera perspective and identification of the peak frame.
  • Sample size: Fifteen participants per population limited generalizability.
  • Single IMU: The final architecture limited direct measurement of three-dimensional relative motion.

Outcome

Project Outcome

The project produced a functional wearable system capable of measuring tibia-over-ankle angle and loaded ankle dorsiflexion ROM. Validation showed an average difference of approximately 1.75° from Kinovea reference measurements.

Population analysis revealed significant ROM differences across nearly all demographic comparisons, with college-aged men demonstrating the highest ROM and both 55+ populations demonstrating substantially lower values. No significant left-right differences were observed.

The project demonstrated the complete development cycle of a wearable biomechanical system, from client need and mechanical design through prototyping, sensor integration, validation, data collection, and statistical analysis.

Future Development

Where the System Could Go Next

Future development could expand the system beyond controlled sagittal-plane dorsiflexion to provide a more complete picture of lower-extremity movement during pickleball.

Multi-Plane Kinematic Measurement

Additional IMUs could measure relative motion across multiple anatomical segments and planes.

Dynamic Pickleball Testing

Future testing could evaluate movement during lateral movement, lunging, cutting, and directional changes.

Expanded Population Studies

Larger and more diverse participant populations would improve the generalizability of the findings.

Longitudinal Monitoring

Repeated measurements could track changes in ankle mobility during training, rehabilitation, or physical therapy.
