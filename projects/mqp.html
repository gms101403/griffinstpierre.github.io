Major Qualifying Project

Pickleball Biomechanics MQP

Development of a wearable biomechanical system for measuring loaded ankle dorsiflexion during pickleball, with the goal of providing useful kinematic data that can help identify potentially concerning movement patterns.

Role

Mechanical / Biomedical Engineering

Project Type

Team-Based MQP

Focus

Biomechanics & Wearable Sensors

Tools

SolidWorks, MATLAB, BNO085 IMU

Overview

Project Overview

Pickleball involves rapid changes in direction, dynamic movements, and repeated loading that may contribute to lower-extremity injury risk. Our project was developed in response to a client request for a non-invasive wearable device capable of providing useful kinematic data to pickleball players that could support injury-prevention efforts.

The original system concept included multiple inertial measurement units (IMUs) and force sensing to capture a broader range of biomechanical measurements during play. As the project progressed, the scope was refined to focus on loaded ankle dorsiflexion as a specific and measurable biomechanical quantity.

The final system used a BNO085 IMU mounted in a custom shank hub and connected to a microcontroller housed within a 3D-printed heel cuff. The system measured the change in tibia-over-ankle angle during a controlled forward-lean movement. A MATLAB-based application processed the sensor data, calculated range of motion, and displayed the resulting measurements.

Beyond developing the wearable system itself, the project investigated how ankle dorsiflexion range of motion varied between college-aged and 55+ men and women and whether meaningful differences existed between the left and right limbs.

Client Need

Design Objective

The client statement that guided the project was: "Design a non-invasive wearable device that provides useful kinematic data for pickleball players to help with injury prevention."

This requirement established two important goals. First, the system needed to provide a practical method for collecting meaningful biomechanical data during movement. Second, the resulting measurements needed to provide information that could be interpreted in the context of potentially concerning movement patterns rather than simply demonstrating that a wearable sensor could collect data.

The final project therefore combined wearable mechanical design, inertial sensing, signal processing, validation, and population-level analysis into a single measurement system.

The Engineering Challenge

From Biomechanical Question to Measurement System

The initial system concept was intentionally broad. The proposed design combined multiple IMUs with force sensing to characterize movement and loading during pickleball. Such a system could potentially provide information across multiple planes of motion and support a broader assessment of movement patterns.

As the project progressed, the scope was narrowed to a specific measurement that could be developed, validated, and meaningfully analyzed within the available project constraints: loaded ankle dorsiflexion.

Loaded ankle dorsiflexion describes the forward progression of the tibia over the foot while the foot remains planted and bearing load. The project used tibia-over-ankle angle as the measurable quantity and calculated range of motion between a neutral standing position and the participant's peak controlled dorsiflexion position.

A target range of approximately 15–30° was used as a reference for evaluating loaded ankle dorsiflexion. Measurements outside this range could identify a potential limitation that may warrant additional mobility work, evaluation, or monitoring. The system was not intended to diagnose injuries or independently determine injury risk.

An important requirement of the final measurement method was that the tested foot remain planted throughout the movement. The participant was required to lean forward over the foot while keeping the heel down and avoiding significant side-to-side movement. This was necessary because the system measured sagittal-plane tibia-over-ankle motion; movement of the foot itself or movement outside the intended plane could change the measured angle and reduce the accuracy of the dorsiflexion calculation.

System Design

Designing the Wearable Measurement System

The wearable system was designed around the need to maintain a consistent geometric relationship between the foot and shank while allowing the participant to perform the controlled dorsiflexion movement.

Proposed System Architecture

The original architecture used two IMUs to measure relative motion between the foot and shank, along with a force-sensing component intended to provide additional loading information. Together, these measurements were intended to characterize movement and loading during pickleball.

Final System Architecture

The finalized system focused on sagittal-plane tibia-over-ankle angle. A BNO085 IMU was mounted within the shank hub while the microcontroller and supporting electronics were housed in the custom heel cuff.

Positioning the components along the lower leg and foot established a consistent reference relationship for the measurement. The orientation data generated by the BNO085 was transmitted for processing in MATLAB, where the signal was filtered and used to determine neutral and peak tibial positions.

A two-IMU configuration would be necessary to directly measure relative motion across all three planes. The final single-IMU configuration was intentionally focused on sagittal-plane dorsiflexion because this was the primary motion being investigated.

Heel Cuff Development

The heel cuff was designed to securely house the microcontroller and associated electronics while maintaining a stable connection to the foot. Because sensor position directly affected measurement consistency, the cuff needed to remain securely positioned during repeated movement.

The design progressed through multiple iterations addressing fit, component separation, wiring access, geometry, manufacturability, and ease of assembly.

Shank Hub Development

The shank hub provided the mounting interface for the BNO085 IMU and established a consistent reference location along the lower leg. Its geometry was developed around the sensor's size and positioning requirements while maintaining a secure connection to the shank.

Multiple iterations addressed strap alignment, enclosure access, attachment, structural stability, and assembly. Prototype feedback was incorporated into subsequent designs to improve the reliability of the sensor mounting interface.

Prototyping & Development

From CAD to Physical Prototype

The mechanical components were developed through an iterative CAD and FDM 3D-printing process. Physical prototypes allowed the team to evaluate fit, component positioning, assembly, structural stability, and sensor integration before beginning the final data collection process.

Prototype testing revealed issues that were difficult to identify in CAD alone. These observations informed changes to component geometry, attachment methods, and sensor positioning before the final system was used for participant testing.

Final Prototype

The completed system integrated the heel cuff, shank hub, BNO085 IMU, and supporting electronics into a wearable assembly. The final configuration was designed to maintain consistent sensor positioning while allowing participants to perform controlled loaded ankle dorsiflexion.

Measurement Method

Measuring Loaded Ankle Dorsiflexion

The primary outcome of the study was ankle dorsiflexion range of motion, defined as the angular change between neutral standing and peak tibial progression over the planted foot during a controlled forward lean.

Standardized Movement

Each participant completed a static standing calibration followed by a controlled dorsiflexion movement. The participant leaned forward over the tested foot as far as possible without lifting the heel and repeated the movement five times. The same procedure was then performed on the opposite foot.

The tested foot needed to remain planted throughout the movement. Participants were instructed to avoid lifting the heel, moving the foot, or leaning substantially to the side. This requirement was important because the final system measured sagittal-plane tibia-over-ankle motion. Additional foot or body movement could introduce motion that was not representative of true ankle dorsiflexion and therefore reduce measurement accuracy.

Sensor Data Processing

The BNO085 IMU recorded orientation data at approximately 100 Hz. The raw orientation data was imported into a custom MATLAB GUI, where filtering was applied to reduce sensor noise and small fluctuations caused by vibration or body sway.

The processing workflow identified the neutral standing angle and the peak forward tibial angle for each trial. Range of motion was then calculated from the angular difference between these two positions.

Data Collection

Comparing Ankle Motion Across Populations

Following development and initial testing of the wearable system, the project expanded into population-level data collection. Measurements were collected from four groups: college-aged men, college-aged women, men age 55+, and women age 55+.

Each participant provided measurements from both the left and right ankles. Five trials were performed for each ankle, allowing the project to calculate an average ROM while also evaluating trial-to-trial repeatability.

College Men

15 participants; mean ROM of 37.8° for the left foot and 36.7° for the right foot.

College Women

15 participants; mean ROM of 30.6° for the left foot and 30.7° for the right foot.

Men 55+

15 participants; mean ROM of 22.3° for the left foot and 21.6° for the right foot.

Women 55+

15 participants; mean ROM of 21.3° for the left foot and 20.7° for the right foot.

Research Questions

Hypotheses & Statistical Analysis

The study used two primary hypotheses to evaluate whether the wearable system could reveal meaningful differences in ankle dorsiflexion and whether measurable asymmetry existed between the limbs.

Hypothesis 1 — Limb Symmetry

Null Hypothesis: Range of motion between the left and right ankles is not statistically significant.

Paired-samples t-tests were performed within each population. All groups produced p-values greater than the significance threshold of α = 0.00833:

  • Men 55+: p = 0.5844
  • Women 55+: p = 0.6402
  • College Men: p = 0.8866
  • College Women: p = 0.8760

These results indicate that no statistically significant differences were observed between left and right ankle ROM within any of the four populations.

Conclusion: The null hypothesis was not rejected. The results support the presence of generally similar dorsiflexion ROM between the left and right limbs within each population.

Hypothesis 2 — Differences in Dorsiflexion ROM

Null Hypothesis: Range of motion between the four populations is not statistically significant.

Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare the populations. Statistical significance was evaluated using α = 0.00833.

Statistically significant differences were observed in every population comparison except the comparison between Men 55+ and Women 55+:

  • College Men vs. College Women: p = 0.0013
  • College Men vs. Women 55+: p < 0.0001
  • College Men vs. Men 55+: p < 0.0001
  • College Women vs. Women 55+: p = 0.0003
  • College Women vs. Men 55+: p = 0.0013
  • Men 55+ vs. Women 55+: p = 0.9235

The results demonstrate that the college-aged male and female populations differed significantly in dorsiflexion ROM, with college-aged men demonstrating the higher mean ROM. Both older populations demonstrated substantially lower ROM values than their college-aged counterparts.

Conclusion: The null hypothesis was rejected because statistically significant differences were found between nearly all population pairs. The exception was the Men 55+ and Women 55+ comparison, which showed no statistically significant difference.

Results

What We Found

The final dataset revealed a clear pattern in ankle dorsiflexion range of motion across age and sex groups. College-aged men demonstrated the highest average ROM, followed by college-aged women. Both 55+ populations demonstrated substantially lower average ROM.

College-aged men demonstrated mean ROM values of 37.8° on the left and 36.7° on the right. College-aged women demonstrated 30.6° on the left and 30.7° on the right. In comparison, men age 55+ averaged 22.3° on the left and 21.6° on the right, while women age 55+ averaged 21.3° and 20.7°, respectively.

These results support the original hypothesis that dorsiflexion ROM would differ between populations. The data also revealed an age-related pattern: the difference between men and women observed in the college-aged groups was no longer statistically significant in the 55+ groups.

In practical terms, the data suggest that the higher dorsiflexion ROM observed in college-aged men relative to college-aged women was accompanied by a substantial reduction in ROM with age. By age 55+, both male and female groups converged toward a similar lower range of dorsiflexion.

Limb Symmetry

Limb symmetry analysis also showed relatively similar left-to-right ROM within each population. Mean symmetry values ranged from 8.98% in college men to 11.17% in college women. The statistical analysis found no significant left-right differences in any group.

This finding is important because it indicates that the population-level differences observed in ROM were not driven by one limb consistently producing a different measurement than the other. Instead, the primary differences were observed between the demographic groups.

Repeatability

Evaluating Trial-to-Trial Consistency

Because each ankle was tested five times, the project was also able to evaluate how consistently the wearable system produced measurements across repeated trials. Coefficients of variation (CV) were calculated for each participant across the five trials.

Mean CV values for the left ankle ranged from 5.90% for college men to 9.15% for college women. For the right ankle, mean CV values ranged from 5.10% for men 55+ to 7.26% for college women.

These results provided an additional measure of system consistency and helped distinguish true differences between populations from variability occurring within repeated measurements.

Validation & Testing

Evaluating Measurement Accuracy

Before using the wearable system for population-level analysis, the measured dorsiflexion values were compared against an independent reference measurement obtained from sagittal-plane video analysis.

Kinovea motion analysis software was used to manually identify anatomical landmarks and determine the ankle angle during the neutral and peak dorsiflexion positions. The resulting measurements were paired with corresponding measurements from the wearable system.

Across the validation measurements, the wearable system demonstrated an average difference of approximately 1.75° from the Kinovea reference measurements.

This result supported the ability of the BNO085-based system to capture changes in tibia-over-ankle angle with reasonable agreement to the independent video-based measurement method.

Validation Limitations

Validation also revealed practical limitations. During some trials, participants moved slightly side-to-side, making it more difficult to identify the exact frame corresponding to peak sagittal-plane dorsiflexion. Camera perspective was another source of potential error, as the foot was not perfectly perpendicular to the camera in every recording.

Data Collection in Practice

Applying the System to Pickleball Players

After validation, the wearable system was used to collect measurements from the four participant populations. The standardized movement allowed measurements to be compared between participants while maintaining the same basic testing conditions.

The final system was not intended to reproduce every movement occurring during live pickleball play. Instead, it provided a controlled method of measuring a specific component of lower-extremity mobility that may be relevant to pickleball movement and injury-prevention research.

Statistical Analysis

Assessing the Data

Normality was evaluated using Q-Q plots for each population and foot. Most datasets were reasonably close to normal distributions, with only minor deviations observed in several groups.

The sample size was 15 participants per population. Although the relatively small sample size limited the ability to fully characterize the underlying population distributions, the absence of extreme outliers and the generally acceptable normality of the datasets supported the use of parametric statistical tests.

Paired-samples t-tests were used to evaluate left-right differences within each population. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare dorsiflexion ROM between populations. A significance threshold of α = 0.00833 was used for the population comparisons.

Engineering Lessons

What I Learned

The project reinforced the importance of developing a measurement system around the limitations of both the sensing technology and the physical testing environment. A theoretically useful measurement is only meaningful when the mechanical system can position the sensor consistently and the testing protocol can control the variables that influence the measurement.

The project also demonstrated the value of iterative engineering development. Mechanical design, prototyping, sensor integration, testing, and data analysis were not independent stages. Findings from each stage influenced decisions made during the next stage of development.

Finally, the population-level analysis demonstrated the importance of separating engineering performance from interpretation of the resulting data. Validating the wearable system established that it could produce useful measurements, while statistical analysis was then necessary to determine whether observed differences between populations were meaningful.

Limitations

System & Study Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of the project.

  • Sagittal-plane measurement: The final system focused on tibia-over-ankle motion in the sagittal plane and did not directly measure relative motion across all three planes.
  • Planted-foot requirement: Accurate measurement depended on the tested foot remaining planted while the participant performed the forward-lean movement. Foot movement or substantial side-to-side motion could introduce measurement error.
  • Controlled movement rather than live play: The testing protocol isolated dorsiflexion using a controlled movement and therefore did not reproduce the full complexity of live pickleball movement.
  • Validation method: Kinovea measurements were affected by camera perspective and difficulty identifying the exact peak frame during trials involving unintended movement.
  • Sample size: Each population contained 15 participants, limiting the ability to generalize the results to the broader population of pickleball players.
  • Single IMU architecture: The final system used one BNO085 IMU for the primary measurement, limiting the system's ability to directly characterize three-dimensional relative motion.

Outcome

Project Outcome

The project resulted in a functional wearable measurement system capable of quantifying tibia-over-ankle angle and calculating loaded ankle dorsiflexion ROM. The system was validated against an independent Kinovea-based measurement, with an average difference of approximately 1.75°, before being used for participant data collection.

Population-level analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences in dorsiflexion ROM across nearly all demographic comparisons. College-aged men demonstrated the highest average ROM, followed by college-aged women, while both 55+ groups demonstrated substantially lower ROM. The difference between men and women was statistically significant among the college-aged participants but was no longer significant in the 55+ population.

No statistically significant left-right differences were identified within any population, supporting the first hypothesis that the measured ROM was generally symmetrical between limbs.

Ultimately, the project demonstrated how a wearable biomechanical system can progress from a client need and broad system concept through mechanical design, rapid prototyping, sensor integration, validation, controlled data collection, and statistical interpretation. The final system provides a foundation for future work involving additional kinematic measurements, more representative pickleball movements, and expanded participant populations.

Future Development

Where the System Could Go Next

Future development could expand the system beyond controlled sagittal-plane dorsiflexion measurements to provide a more complete picture of lower-extremity movement during pickleball.

Multi-Plane Kinematic Measurement

Additional IMUs could be incorporated to measure relative motion across multiple anatomical segments and planes, allowing the system to characterize a broader range of movement patterns.

Dynamic Pickleball Testing

Future testing could evaluate dorsiflexion and other kinematic variables during actual pickleball movements, including lateral movement, lunging, cutting, and directional changes.

Expanded Population Studies

A larger and more diverse participant population would allow the observed age- and sex-related trends to be investigated more thoroughly and improve the generalizability of the findings.

Longitudinal Monitoring

Repeated measurements over time could allow the system to track changes in ankle mobility during training, rehabilitation, or physical therapy.

Integrated Injury-Prevention Tool

With additional validated measurements and clinical or biomechanical reference data, future versions could provide more comprehensive feedback to users about movement characteristics that may warrant further assessment.
