Pickleball Biomechanics MQP

Development and validation of a wearable biomechanical system for measuring loaded ankle dorsiflexion during controlled pickleball-related movement.

Overview

Project Overview

Pickleball involves repeated acceleration, deceleration, changes in direction, and loaded lower-extremity movement. Our project investigated whether a wearable system could provide useful kinematic data to help players better understand movement characteristics associated with potential injury risk.

The project was developed in response to the client statement: Design a non-invasive wearable device that provides useful kinematic data for pickleball players to help with injury prevention.

The original concept included multiple inertial measurement units (IMUs) and force sensing to capture a broader range of biomechanical variables. As the project progressed, the scope was refined to focus on loaded ankle dorsiflexion, measured as the change in tibia-over-ankle angle between a neutral standing position and peak forward tibial progression.

The final system combined a BNO085 IMU mounted in a custom shank hub with a FeatherSense-based heel cuff and a MATLAB-based processing and visualization application. The system was validated against sagittal-plane video measurements using Kinovea and was then used to investigate differences in dorsiflexion range of motion between limbs, sex groups, and age groups.

The Engineering Challenge

From Injury-Prevention Goal to a Measurable Biomechanical Variable

The client requested a non-invasive wearable system capable of providing useful kinematic information to pickleball players. Translating that broad objective into a measurable engineering quantity required narrowing the problem to a movement that could be measured reliably with a wearable sensor.

The team selected loaded ankle dorsiflexion as the primary measurement. Loaded dorsiflexion describes the forward progression of the tibia over the foot while the foot remains planted and bearing load. Limited dorsiflexion has been associated with altered movement strategies and may be a relevant factor when evaluating lower-extremity movement.

A target range of approximately 15–30° was used as a reference for evaluating loaded ankle dorsiflexion. The device was not intended to diagnose injuries or directly determine injury risk. Instead, the goal was to provide objective kinematic information that could help identify movement characteristics warranting further assessment, mobility work, or other preventative strategies.

Measurement Constraint

A critical requirement of the final measurement method was maintaining a planted foot during the movement. The foot wearing the device must remain planted while the participant bends forward primarily through the ankle. Side-to-side movement, lifting the heel, or otherwise moving the foot changes the measured orientation and can introduce motion that is not representative of true sagittal-plane ankle dorsiflexion.

Participants were therefore instructed to begin from a neutral standing position and lean forward over the planted foot as far as possible without lifting the heel. They could use their preferred forward-lean technique, but were instructed not to lean laterally or otherwise move the foot because doing so could skew the resulting ankle-angle measurement.

System Design

Designing the Wearable Measurement System

The system was designed around a simple measurement principle: by establishing a consistent reference between the foot and shank, the change in orientation between a neutral position and peak forward tibial progression could be used to estimate loaded ankle dorsiflexion.

Proposed System Architecture

The original system concept used two IMUs and a force-sensing component to capture multiple biomechanical characteristics during pickleball. This broader architecture was intended to provide information about movement and loading across multiple planes.

Final System Architecture

The final system was narrowed to sagittal-plane measurement of tibia-over-ankle angle. A BNO085 IMU was mounted on the shank hub while the microcontroller and supporting electronics were housed in the heel cuff.

The BNO085 combines accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer measurements through onboard sensor-fusion algorithms to provide orientation data. The orientation data was recorded at approximately 100 Hz and exported for processing in MATLAB.

Positioning the sensor along the shank and maintaining a consistent relationship between the shank hub and heel cuff allowed the system to estimate the change in tibial orientation relative to the foot without requiring complex geometric calculations.

Mechanical Development

Designing the Wearable Hardware

Heel Cuff Development

The heel cuff was designed to house the electronics while maintaining a stable connection to the foot during movement. Because sensor position directly affects the measurement, the cuff needed to maintain a repeatable relationship with the foot without unnecessarily restricting natural motion.

The heel cuff progressed through multiple iterations addressing component separation, fit, wiring access, geometry, and manufacturability. Physical prototypes were used to identify issues that were difficult to evaluate in CAD alone.

Shank Hub Development

The shank hub provided the mounting interface for the BNO085 IMU and established a consistent reference location along the lower leg. Its geometry was developed around the sensor's size, orientation, attachment requirements, and need to remain stable during movement.

Several iterations addressed strap alignment, enclosure access, attachment, structural stability, and assembly. Prototype observations were incorporated into subsequent designs to improve the reliability of the sensor mounting interface.

Prototyping & Development

From CAD to Physical Prototype

The finalized mechanical designs were converted into physical prototypes using FDM 3D printing. Prototyping allowed the team to evaluate fit, sensor positioning, attachment, assembly, and stability before completing the measurement system.

Physical testing revealed issues that were difficult to identify in CAD alone, including adjustments needed for component fit, attachment, and sensor positioning. These observations were incorporated into subsequent revisions before the final system was used for participant testing.

Final Prototype

The completed system integrated the heel cuff, shank hub, BNO085 IMU, and supporting electronics into a wearable assembly. The final configuration was designed to maintain consistent sensor positioning while allowing participants to perform controlled loaded ankle dorsiflexion.

Measurement Methods

Converting IMU Orientation into Ankle ROM

The primary outcome was ankle dorsiflexion range of motion (ROM), defined as the angular change between a neutral standing position and peak tibial progression over the ankle during a controlled forward lean.

Data Acquisition

During each trial, the BNO085 IMU recorded orientation data at approximately 100 Hz. The recorded time-series data included timestamps and Euler-angle measurements that were exported for processing in MATLAB.

MATLAB Processing

A custom MATLAB GUI was developed to process the raw orientation data. Filtering was used to reduce sensor noise and small fluctuations caused by vibration or body sway. A low-pass filter was applied to the roll-angle signal to isolate the smooth forward tibial rotation associated with dorsiflexion.

The processing workflow identified two key points within each trial: the neutral angle recorded during relaxed standing and the peak angle reached during maximum controlled dorsiflexion. The difference between these angles was used to calculate the participant's dorsiflexion ROM.

Testing Protocol

Standardizing the Dorsiflexion Measurement

Participants wore the heel cuff and shank hub while completing a standardized controlled dorsiflexion task. The protocol was designed to isolate sagittal-plane ankle motion while maintaining a planted foot.

  1. Place the heel cuff and shank hub on the participant.
  2. Record the static standing angle for calibration.
  3. Lean forward over the planted foot as far as possible without lifting the heel.
  4. Record the peak dorsiflexion angle.
  5. Repeat the movement five times for the tested ankle.
  6. Repeat the same procedure for the opposite ankle.

Participants were allowed to use their preferred forward-lean technique to reach peak dorsiflexion. However, they were instructed not to lean laterally or move the foot during the trial because those movements could introduce non-sagittal motion and reduce measurement accuracy.

Testing was performed indoors on a flat court surface to approximate typical pickleball conditions while maintaining a controlled environment.

Validation & Testing

Validating the Wearable Measurement

Before using the system for population-level analysis, the IMU-derived dorsiflexion measurements were compared against an independent reference measurement obtained from sagittal- plane video analysis using Kinovea.

During validation trials, the wearable system and a video camera recorded the same dorsiflexion movement. Kinovea was then used to manually identify anatomical landmarks and measure the ankle angle at the corresponding neutral and peak positions.

Validation Result

Across the validation measurements, the IMU-derived angle differed from the Kinovea reference by an average of approximately 1.75°. This supported the ability of the wearable system to capture meaningful changes in tibia-over-ankle angle for the controlled measurement task.

The validation also highlighted practical limitations. Side-to-side movement during some trials made it more difficult to identify the exact peak dorsiflexion position in the video, while camera perspective that was not perfectly perpendicular to the sagittal plane could introduce additional measurement error.

Data Collection

Investigating Dorsiflexion Across Age and Sex

After validating the measurement approach, the system was used to collect bilateral ankle dorsiflexion measurements from four participant populations: college-aged men, college-aged women, men age 55+, and women age 55+.

Each participant completed five trials per ankle. The five trials were averaged to obtain a mean dorsiflexion ROM for each ankle, which was then used for group-level analysis.

Study Hypotheses

The first hypothesis examined whether participants demonstrated meaningful left-to-right differences in ankle ROM. The null hypothesis stated that ROM between the left and right ankles would not be statistically significant.

The second hypothesis examined whether dorsiflexion ROM differed between the four populations. The null hypothesis stated that ROM between the populations would not be statistically significant.

Results

What the Data Showed

The results showed clear differences in dorsiflexion ROM between college-aged and 55+ participants, while left-to-right differences within groups were not statistically significant. The data also revealed an interaction between age and sex: college-aged men demonstrated the greatest ROM, college-aged women were lower, and both older groups decreased to similar ranges.

Range of Motion by Population

College-aged men had the highest average dorsiflexion ROM, with mean values of 37.8° for the left ankle and 36.7° for the right ankle. College-aged women averaged 30.6° on the left and 30.7° on the right.

The older groups demonstrated substantially lower ROM. Men 55+ averaged 22.3° on the left and 21.6° on the right, while women 55+ averaged 21.3° on the left and 20.7° on the right.

These results indicate that the largest differences occurred between age groups. The sex-based difference that was present in the college-aged population was no longer statistically significant in the 55+ population, where men and women demonstrated similar mean ROM.

Hypothesis 1 — Limb Symmetry

Paired-samples t-tests were used to compare mean dorsiflexion ROM between the left and right ankles within each population. All four groups produced p-values greater than the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.00833.

Age and Sex Trends

The population results suggest that dorsiflexion ROM was generally higher among the college-aged participants, with college-aged men showing the greatest average ROM. College-aged women also demonstrated higher ROM than both older populations.

In contrast, the 55+ groups converged toward a similar range. Men 55+ averaged approximately 21.9° overall, while women 55+ averaged approximately 21.0°. The difference between these groups was only 0.96° and was not statistically significant.

These findings support the broader observation that the sex difference in dorsiflexion ROM present among the college-aged participants became much smaller with age, while overall ROM decreased substantially in both groups.

Repeatability

Trial-to-trial repeatability was evaluated using the coefficient of variation across five trials for each ankle. Mean CV values ranged from approximately 5.1% to 9.15% across the four populations and two limbs.

The lowest mean CV was observed for the right ankle of the Men 55+ group at 5.10%, while the highest mean CV occurred for the left ankle of the College Women group at 9.15%. Individual CV values varied more widely, indicating that repeatability was influenced by participant and trial conditions.

Statistical Assumptions

Q-Q plots were used to visually assess normality for each group. Most datasets followed the expected reference pattern, with only minor deviations observed in some groups. Because the analysis used mean values averaged across five trials per participant, the resulting distributions were also less influenced by individual trial variability.

With 15 participants per population, no extreme outliers, and only minor deviations from normality, parametric t-tests were considered appropriate for the analyses performed.

Interpretation

Connecting the Measurements to the Engineering Goal

The results demonstrate how a wearable measurement system can move beyond simply collecting sensor data and provide information that can be interpreted at the population level. The system identified meaningful differences in loaded ankle dorsiflexion across the tested age and sex groups while also showing no statistically significant left-to-right differences within any individual population.

The observed reduction in ROM with age was particularly notable. College-aged men and women showed substantially greater dorsiflexion ROM than the corresponding 55+ populations, while the difference between older men and women became statistically insignificant. This suggests that age may have a stronger relationship with the measured ROM than sex within the older population tested.

These findings do not establish a direct causal relationship between dorsiflexion ROM and injury occurrence. Instead, they demonstrate the potential of the wearable system to collect objective kinematic information that could be used to identify differences, monitor changes over time, and support further investigation of movement characteristics relevant to pickleball injury prevention.

Limitations

Measurement and Study Limitations

The measurement approach was intentionally focused on controlled sagittal-plane dorsiflexion rather than unrestricted movement during live pickleball play. This improved consistency but means the measured ROM does not capture every movement strategy used during gameplay.

Because the foot must remain planted and the motion must occur primarily through the ankle, participant technique can affect measurement quality. Lateral movement, foot movement, or heel lift can introduce additional orientation changes that are not representative of the intended dorsiflexion measurement.

The validation procedure also depended on video perspective and manual identification of peak motion. Side-to-side movement made some peaks difficult to identify, while imperfect camera alignment could introduce error into the Kinovea reference measurement.

Finally, the population analysis included 15 participants in each group. The results therefore provide evidence of trends within the tested sample but should not be interpreted as establishing universal normative values for all pickleball players.

Outcome

Project Outcome

The project resulted in a validated wearable system capable of measuring tibia-over-ankle angle during controlled loaded dorsiflexion. The BNO085-based system produced measurements that differed from the Kinovea reference by an average of approximately 1.75°, providing evidence that the wearable approach could capture meaningful changes in ankle position.

The final system was then used to investigate bilateral symmetry and differences in dorsiflexion ROM across age and sex groups. No statistically significant left-to-right differences were found within any population, while significant differences were found across nearly all population pairings. College-aged men demonstrated the greatest ROM, while both 55+ groups decreased to similar lower ranges.

Beyond the physical prototype, the project provided experience taking a broad client need through system definition, mechanical design, wearable sensor integration, data processing, validation, participant testing, and statistical analysis. The work demonstrated how engineering design decisions and experimental results can be connected to a broader biomedical application.
